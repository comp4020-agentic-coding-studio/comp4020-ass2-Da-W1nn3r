# Factorio: Engineering Addiction

Week 11 — Megabase Economics

---

## Megabases and travel time

A megabase isn't just a bigger base — distance itself becomes a cost, and
the layout has to be chosen to pay that cost as little as possible.

- station throughput isn't the same as train capacity on paper: loading
  time, unloading time, travel time and station recovery time all eat into
  how much a route actually moves per hour
- a hub-and-spoke layout (many small mining outposts feeding a few
  concentration hubs, which then run fewer, larger trains to the main base)
  cuts down congestion at the receiving end compared to every outpost
  running its own train straight into the base
- a grid-shaped rail network is easy to expand outward, but a grid rarely
  gives the shortest path between two points — expandability and travel
  time are pulling in different directions, not the same one

---

## TPS basics

Every Factorio tick has a fixed budget — about 16.667 milliseconds — to
finish every calculation the simulation owes for that tick, and once a
base is big enough, that budget stops being free.

- the game targets 60 updates per second (UPS); if the work due in a tick
  takes longer than its budget, UPS drops below 60 and the whole simulation
  runs in slow motion, not just the part that was slow
- entity count is usually the largest single cost, and for assembling
  machines specifically it's the number of machines that costs UPS, not
  their speed — fewer, faster machines (via modules and beacons) are
  cheaper to simulate than many slow ones producing the same output
- belts, trains, fluid networks and electric networks are each their own
  cost line, and electric network cost scales with how many separate
  networks exist, not how big any one of them is — one stray disconnected
  power pole is its own costly network

## Expansion

Growing a factory has a theoretical ceiling: if every belt of plate a base
produces is fed straight back into building more production, output can
roughly double every five to six minutes — but only while there's still
somewhere useful to place the next machine.

- doubling requires both the materials to build the next stage and the
  time to actually place it — a factory that has the plates but not the
  belts, poles, or bot coverage to place them isn't expanding at the
  theoretical rate, it's expanding at the placement rate
- expansion has a stopping point set by the goal, not by momentum: a
  rocket launch needs a specific, finite amount of tech and rocket
  parts, so growing the "factory that builds the factory" past what's
  needed for that number just spends time that should have gone to the
  goal itself
- this is the same tension as the megabase travel-time tradeoff above,
  one level up: every layout decision either buys throughput or buys
  time to build, never both for free

---

## Speedrunning

A speedrun optimises for one number — wall-clock time to a rocket launch —
and that single target changes which designs count as good, compared to a
base built to run indefinitely.

- a speedrun base is judged on how fast it reaches the tech and parts a
  rocket needs, not on how efficiently it uses raw resources afterward —
  overproducing anything costs time without buying anything back
- because the run ends, long-term concerns a permanent base has to solve —
  UPS at scale, sustainable ore supply, tileable expansion — mostly don't
  apply; a speedrun base can be deliberately wasteful anywhere that
  wastefulness doesn't cost time
- the core speedrun decision is exactly the expansion-versus-goal tradeoff
  above, but compressed: how long to keep growing the factory that builds
  the factory before switching every resource toward the rocket itself

---

## Designing blueprints for speed

The same blueprint can be optimised for two different things — how fast it
goes up, or how much it produces once it's finished — and a design that's
good at one is often not the design that's good at the other.

- a build-speed blueprint favours fewer, simpler entities a bot can place
  quickly and few wiring/circuit dependencies to check afterward; a
  throughput blueprint favours whatever combination of machines, modules
  and beacons produces the most per tile, even if it's slower to place
- fewer, faster machines cost less UPS for the same output than many slow
  ones — so a throughput-and-UPS-optimised blueprint and a naive
  "just add more assemblers" blueprint can hit the same production number
  while costing very different amounts of simulation time
- neither design is wrong — they're solving different problems, which is
  exactly the comparison this week's practical asks for

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> A speedrun spends time to save time later. A megabase spends layout to save time forever. Neither one is optimising for "more output" in the abstract — ask what a design is actually paying for before calling it good.

## This week's practical

```txt
Goal: given two base designs aimed at different goals, explain the purpose
      of their differences
Test: identify what each design is actually optimising for, then trace
      specific design choices back to that target
Pass: each design's actual optimisation target is correctly identified; at
      least three specific design choices are tied back to that target;
      the comparison doesn't just declare one design "better" overall
```

"Better" only means something once you've said better *at what* — a
speedrun base and a megabase can both be correct designs for their own
goal and wrong designs for the other one.
